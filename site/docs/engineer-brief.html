What it is

Projector is a curator-led film companion. A film programmer picks a film, sets a showtime, and hosts a screening. The audience watches on their own television, on whatever service they already pay for, and keeps Projector open on a phone, where the curator's notes arrive in sync alongside live chat with everyone else in the room.

Pop-Up Video, live, with a film programmer instead of a producer.

Founded by James DeJulio, CEO of Tongal, and Michael Martin, who built and ran the accelerator at Ava Labs. We are running a proof of concept this autumn with five curators and fifteen ticketed screenings, then raising against it.

The interesting technical problem

We never touch the film. No player, no embed, no streaming rights, no platform integration. The viewer presses play on their own Netflix or MUBI at an agreed moment, and everything we do runs off a shared start time.

That one decision is the whole architecture. It means every film ever made is in the catalogue on day one and we can launch without a single rights deal. It also means the session clock is the product. If it drifts, a note about the cheongsams fires while someone is watching the credits, and the illusion that a hundred people are in a room together collapses.

So the work is: keep a clock honest across a hundred phones for two hours, with no telemetry from the thing everyone is actually watching, when some of those phones lock, background, and come back.

What you would own

This is the spine. A non-engineer founder is building the screens, with coding agents, in the repository you set up. You are building the parts that have to be right, and you are the person who reads what the agents produced.

1. The repository and the guardrails. First thing, before any screen exists. Supabase project, schema, migrations, row-level security policies, CI, deploy, environments and secrets, and a CLAUDE.md carrying the spec and the design rules so that everything built afterwards is built inside constraints you set. Google OAuth for curators, anonymous sessions for the audience with identity linking on optional sign-in. You are not auditing someone else's mistakes at the midpoint; you are making them harder to commit.

2. The session clock. Server-authoritative elapsed time from a shared start. Survives a locked phone, a backgrounded tab, and a reconnect. Clients resync rather than drift.

3. The note-firing engine. Notes fire when the clock crosses their mark, identically for everyone, with a manual override for the curator and a mid-session edit path. It also drives a rehearsal mode where a curator watches her own session at eight times speed with no audience.

4. Chat and presence over the same realtime channel.

5. Intermission. Pausing the clock for the room, a countdown, and an "I've paused my TV" tap that tells the curator how much of the room is actually with her. We cannot pause anyone's television, which is a known and accepted limitation; the job is to make the social contract work with good instrumentation.

6. Ticketing. $0.99 taken at reserve, the seat held only once payment clears, a refund path. Record what each curator is owed. Do not build Stripe Connect — we pay five people by transfer.

7. Failure states and data integrity. What a curator sees when a save fails, an upload fails, or the connection drops mid-session. Whether she can lose an evening of notes. What two open tabs do. Generated code does not write any of this unprompted and it is the difference between a demo and a product.

8. Adversarial review of everything else. Most of the application is written fast, by agents, by a founder who is not an engineer. It will be plausible and it will be wrong somewhere. Finding where is a deliverable, not a courtesy, and it is budgeted as one below.

The stack

Supabase for Postgres, Auth and Realtime. Row-level security throughout. React front end, written in the repository with coding agents against an existing design token sheet, so expect ordinary code of uneven quality rather than a hosted builder's output. Stripe for payments. Deployed on Vercel or equivalent.

If you have a strong opinion that some of this is the wrong choice, we want to hear it in the first conversation rather than the third week.

Shape of the engagement

About twelve working days across ten weeks, mid September to late November, in three unequal pieces rather than one continuous block:

  • Three days at the start, before anything else is built. The repository, schema, migrations, RLS policies, CI, deploy, environments and secrets.
  • Four days on the spine. The clock, the firing engine, the realtime channel, intermission, ticketing.
  • Five days of review, spread across the whole build. Read the diffs. Assume something is confidently wrong and find it.

The third block is the one to argue with us about if you think it is the wrong shape. It is deliberately not banked at the end, because a review that arrives at the end inherits decisions nobody can afford to unwind.

Part-time is fine and probably better. Remote, with Michael in New Orleans on Central time.

What you get that most contract briefs do not

An eleven-section product specification with acceptance criteria per sprint, eleven wireframe artboards covering every screen, a written design language with an oklch token sheet that is already code, and a sprint plan with dates. Read them before the first call:

  • Wireframes: https://projector-mm.netlify.app/wireframes/
  • The spec and sprint plan: sent on request

We know what we are building. What we do not have is someone who can make the clock honest.

Explicitly not in scope

The AI research agent, screenshot and natural-language timestamping, the telestrator, the Marquee, push notifications, and any streaming-platform integration. All of it is specced and none of it is this engagement.

What to send back

Not a CV. Three things:

  1. Something you have built where timing or synchronisation across clients actually mattered, and what broke
  2. How you would set up row-level security for this, given a founder and a set of agents will be writing against it for three months without you watching every commit
  3. Your read on the session clock: where you would put the source of truth, and how you would handle a phone that locks at minute forty and comes back at minute fifty-five

The third one is the job. If it is interesting to you, we should talk.
