Estimate written 2026-08-21, rewritten 2026-08-28 against how the build actually happens: in a git repository with coding agents, not in Lovable. Scope is unchanged. Numbers are not.

The short answer

About 35 hours of Michael and about twelve days of a contract engineer, for everything needed to run a real screening. Curator tools, the audience side, ticketing, and the two phone layouts.

The previous version of this document said 100 hours and fifteen engineer days. Before that, 55 to 75. The 55 to 75 was wrong on scope and the 100 was wrong on method, and the second error is the interesting one: it priced construction as though someone were typing it. In the mode this is actually built in, construction is close to free and the cost has moved entirely into deciding what is right and proving it.

The research agent and AI timestamping are still cut and are not in any number below.

Where the new numbers come from

Ruskin is the calibration, because it was built the way this one will be. First commit 27 August at 09:42. The publish pipeline, with a lock, a network check, a freshness assertion, a deploy-repo check and a secret scanner, shipped at 17:10 the same day. That is about seven and a half hours to a deployed application of roughly five thousand lines: a crawler across nineteen publications, a scoring model with seven dimension weights and forty-six band scores, an installable web app with its own design language, a 996-line test suite, and the publish path. Two further hours the next morning on the top bar and the corner dock.

Estimated the way the 21 August version of this document estimated, Ruskin is a forty to sixty hour project. It took eight. The factor is not uniform, though, and treating it as uniform is how the next estimate goes wrong in the other direction.

What compresses by four or five times. Screens built from a specification that already exists, against a design system that already exists. Forms, routing, CRUD, layout, the promo kit, the design language pass. This is precisely what Ruskin was.

What compresses by two. Schema and row-level security. Anything on the realtime spine. The writing is fast; the reviewing is not, and an RLS policy that is wrong is wrong silently.

What barely compresses. Integration against somebody else's running code, which is the dry run. Reactive fixing across a live beta, which is demand-driven rather than typing-driven.

What does not compress at all. Five curators using the tool for two weeks. A clock tested against real phones that really lock and really come back. Stripe account verification. Michael's calendar.

The shape matters more than the total. Half of what is left sits in three lines: the dry run, the Room, and reactive fixes. Those are the ones where the work is judgement and multi-client verification rather than construction, and they are the ones to protect when a sprint runs long. Row-level security is still the line most likely to double.

Spread across six sprints at roughly 32 hours of capacity each, the old plan ran 18, 22, 18, 29, 10 and 5, with sprint four scraping its ceiling. At the new numbers it runs roughly 7, 6, 7, 9, 4 and 2. Sprint four stops being the constraint. Nothing in the plan is capacity-bound any more, which changes what the plan is for: it is now a calendar for recruiting curators, testing with them, and raising, with the build fitted around it rather than the reverse.

That is nine days. Do not book nine days.

What the engineer is being paid for is judgement about a clock that has to be right in front of a hundred people, and the nine-day figure is what it costs him to type the answer, not to know it. Cutting to nine buys less review, and review is the whole product of the engagement. So keep the budget at twelve days and move the work:

  • Three days up front, before Michael builds anything. Repository, schema, migrations, row-level security policies, CI, deploy, environments and secrets, and a CLAUDE.md carrying the spec and the design rules. This is the same three days the old plan spent auditing, spent earlier and spent building guardrails instead of finding out what went wrong inside them.
  • Four days on the spine. Clock, firing engine, realtime channel, intermission, ticketing.
  • Five days of adversarial review, spread across the whole build rather than banked at the end. Read what the agents produced. Assume it is confidently wrong somewhere and find where.

Build the charge, not the payout. No Stripe Connect. Record what is owed and pay the five curators by transfer. Automated payouts are worth building at fifty curators, not five.

Why a repository rather than Lovable

The full argument, including Conductor as a possible shell and its limits, is in Projector-Build-Environment.md. The short version:

The 27 August sprint plan already said the engineer has to build the realtime spine "because the session clock and the note-firing engine are the parts Lovable produces convincingly and wrongly." That is a statement about a ceiling, and it is the reason to leave.

Confidently wrong is the worst output available here, and there is a live example in this vault. On 2026-08-27 Ruskin's bin/build.mjs threw a ReferenceError on its first article, printed the stack trace, and exited 0. Nothing flagged it. The feed would have gone stale silently. That was caught because the output was checked rather than the exit code, and the fix is now a guard in the publish path. None of that is available inside a hosted prompt-to-app builder: there is no test runner to hold the guard and no diff to read.

Three other things follow from a repository. The engineer inherits an ordinary codebase instead of generated React he has to correct, which is cheaper for him and removes the hand-off entirely. The design system can live as code rather than as a prompt, which is the subject of the next section. And there is no eject moment between the beta and whatever comes after it.

The wireframes already prove the mode works for this project specifically: they are thirteen hand-built artboards with a real token sheet, produced in the same way.

The design language stops being a risk and becomes an asset

The old plan carried a warning that the design pass is the thing that gets cut when a sprint runs long, and a rule that it must never be cut. Both still stand. What changes is that the pass is no longer six to ten hours of fighting a prompt.

The design system already exists as code. projector/wireframes/_kit.txt holds the whole of it: the four faces, the oklch tokens for the two lighting states, and the component classes down to .livedot. DESIGN.md holds the rules and, more usefully, the don'ts. Between them there is more specification here than most funded teams have.

So the pass is a compile step rather than a creative one. Lift _kit.txt into the application's stylesheet as the single source of truth, build every screen against those tokens, and make the wireframe bodies the visual reference the work is checked against. The two lighting states, the serif as the curator's voice and nowhere else, and the chat rule are then enforced by the tokens rather than remembered in a prompt.

The one thing that must be set up before any screen is built is the visual feedback loop: Playwright MCP, so the agent can take a screenshot and compare it to the artboard. Without it the design work is being done blind, and blind is where generic output comes from. Setup is part of the engineer's first three days. Which skills to run and which to keep away from this build are in Projector-Build-Environment.md.

The calendar is your real constraint, and now it is the only one

At 35 hours across twelve weeks, the build is no longer what sets the date. Recruiting five curators, getting fifteen dates on a calendar, running the screenings, and measuring return rate are. That was always true and the old hours obscured it.

The lever that remains, if the close date matters, is still scope rather than speed, and the cut order is in the sprint plan.

Hire the engineer before you start, not at sixty percent and not at the end

The 21 August version said bring them in at around sixty percent. That was right against a hundred hours of Michael's work spread over three months. Against 35 hours it is wrong, and the same argument now points earlier: two or three hours of a good engineer on the schema and the auth model changes what gets built next, and at this speed "next" arrives in days rather than weeks. An engineer who arrives at sixty percent of a fast build arrives at the end of it.

So: hire in sprint one, start in sprint one. Their first three days are the repository and the guardrails. Everything Michael builds after that is built inside them.

The thing that makes this cheaper than it should be

You have an eleven-section product spec with acceptance criteria per sprint, thirteen wireframe artboards, a written design language, and a token sheet. Almost nobody hands a contract engineer that, and almost nobody hands an agent that either. It is why the numbers above are what they are. Put it in the job post and in front of the engineer in the first conversation. It shortens their ramp, it tells you quickly whether they read things, and it is worth real money in a fixed-price quote.
