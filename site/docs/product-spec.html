Product Specification · Updated 29 August 2026 · Film only · Internal

The product is Projector. The person running a screening is a projectionist, which is what the curator is called throughout this document and in the UI.

The build target is a git repository worked with coding agents. See Projector-Sprint-Plan.md for the calendar and Projector-Build-Plan.md for the hours.

1. Product overview

Projector is a curator-led film companion. A curator picks a film, researches it, writes notes pinned to specific moments, and hosts a session at a set time. The audience watches the film on whatever service they already pay for, on their own TV, and keeps Projector open on a second screen where the curator's Liner Notes arrive in sync alongside live chat with everyone else in the room.

The reference: Pop-Up Video, live, with a film programmer instead of a producer and a room full of people instead of a solo viewer.

The product never embeds or controls the film. It has no player, no streaming rights, and no platform integration. It runs on a shared start time and a session clock, which is why it works against Netflix, MUBI, Criterion, a rented file, or a disc, with no partnership required.

User types.

  • Curator. Has an account, Google sign-in only. Researches films, writes Liner Notes, sets showtimes, hosts the room. Also called a projectionist.
  • Audience member. Arrives on a link. No account required to reserve a seat or to attend. Optional sign-in unlocks following curators, push notifications, and a Taste Passport.

2. Scope

V1, the curator beta

Everything a curator needs to author a session and confirm it works, with no audience present. Five design partners use it for roughly two weeks before any public screening.

In scope. Curator account and sign-in. Session creation. The note editor with manual mm:ss entry, body text and an attached still. The note queue with intermissions. The promo kit with per-channel tracked links. Session ready and the share link. The dry run.

Cut from V1 and not before the raise. The research agent (6.2, 6.4) and AI timestamping by screenshot or natural language (6.5, ways two and three). Both are specced in full because they are the next thing built, and neither is needed to test whether a curator can author a night. Manual mm:ss is the base path and it is sufficient.

Also not in V1: the telestrator, ask other experts, and quote check.

V2, the audience app

Onboarding, the Marquee, the session page and reserve a seat, the Lobby, the calls, the leader, the Room, Liner Note delivery, chat, intermission, reel change, the pre-show, Lights up, and the recap. Built in sprint four, alongside ticketing: $0.99 taken at reserve, with the founding curators keeping all of it, and Apple Pay and Google Pay at checkout.

The curator profile (7.7) is V2 but late. It is the only new surface in the audience app and the link previews behind it are a background job, so it lands after a screening can run end to end. It is also where the promo kit's tracked links eventually want to point, which is the argument for building it rather than deferring it indefinitely.

Specced in full below because the two tracks share a data model, and because the curator tools are meaningless without knowing what they produce.

Out of scope entirely

Music and DJ sessions, cut on the 6 August call and not deferred. Advertising, although the sponsored slot keeps reserved space (see 6.6). Co-curator roles, cinephile badging and the expert bank. Any corporate or funding structure. Any streaming platform partnership or player integration.

4. Core loop

  1. Curator picks a film and writes from what she knows. Later: Research the film returns a structured brief, and ask other experts sends the film out to people whose taste she trusts. Neither is in the first build.
  2. Curator writes Liner Notes, each pinned to a moment in the film, pulling from the brief, from what came back from their contacts, and from their own knowledge.
  3. Curator sets intermissions, arms the queue, sets a showtime, and gets a share link.
  4. Curator posts the link. Audience members reserve seats, and on the way in they answer two prompts: what they would add, and what they want to ask.
  5. Doors open at T-30. The house calls at T-10 and T-2. At T-1 the curtain appears behind the chat, and at T-25 seconds it begins to part. The leader runs on the revealed surface from T-15 seconds. On zero the leader ends, the sign ignites, everyone presses play on their own screen, and the session clock starts.
  6. Liner Notes fire on their marks. Chat runs throughout. Intermissions pause the clock. Anyone who drifts hits resync.
  7. The film ends and the house lights come up. Chat stays open five minutes while the room talks, and the curator can hand everyone off to a live stream. Then the recap, holding the notes, the Film DNA, and what she is running next.

6. Curator tools

What is in the first build and what is not. V1 is 6.1, 6.5 with manual entry only, 6.6, 6.7, 6.8, 6.9 and 6.10. Deferred until after the raise: the research agent (6.2), findings to note (6.4), ask other experts (6.3), quote check and AI timestamping inside 6.5, and the telestrator. They are specced in full because they are the next thing built.

These are desktop screens. Designed at 1440 by 900, drawn that way in the wireframes, and not responsive down to a phone; below about 1100px they show a come-back-on-a-laptop message rather than a squeezed layout. The two exceptions are Go live (6.7) and On air (6.8), which a curator may run from a phone while the film plays on her television, and which are drawn at phone size in the wireframes as well. Go live lands in sprint three and On air in sprint four. See sections 9 and 10.

6.1 Prep home (1J①)

The curator's session workspace. Header carries the film, the showtime, and the seat count. Three primary actions, in order:

  • Research the film. No subtitle. The words "brief the agent" are cut, per James. The AI component is understood and does not need labelling.
  • Ask other experts. Sits directly beneath Research the film, which is where James pictured it. It is also reachable from the research agent screen (6.2), since that is where a curator notices a gap.
  • Write your notes, showing the count set so far.
  • Go live, disabled until the queue has at least one armed note and a showtime.

Where you are going afterwards. A small field, not a primary action: the platform she will go live on when the film ends, and her handle. It defaults to whatever she used last time, so most sessions never touch it. It builds the URL where a permanent one exists and takes a raw link where one does not, and it is what the handoff banner carries at minute three (7.4). Empty is fine and the banner simply does not fire.

6.2 Research agent (1J②) · deferred

Scope chips, unchanged from the wireframes: locations, above the line, below the line, cast, period and context. A free-text prompt field, then Run deep research.

The agent runs once per film on request, pulls only from sources the curator has approved during a one-time source discovery step, returns a structured brief with a source link on every claim, quotes short enough to sit inside fair use, and publishes nothing on its own. Target turnaround under five minutes.

The brief returns in five parts: what is documented, what critics said on release, what is said about it now, novel or contested findings, and suggested Liner Note moments with a proposed timestamp and a one-line draft.

Ask other experts appears here as well. James's framing was that the AI and the human channel answer different questions: "on one hand it's asking Spielberg, the name of our AI, or ask your buddy who also probably knows more about In the Mood for Love than Spielberg does."

6.3 Ask other experts (1J②) · deferred

When built, it is a compose and send out flow, not an in-network feature. The curator writes a short ask, the product generates a link, and the curator sends it through whatever channel they already use. James named the channel: "I think Instagram is the kinda right place for something like this. Can I ping these other people that I talk to on it? Because I chat message my friends on Instagram."

  • Compose screen prefills with the film and a default ask, editable: "I'm programming In the Mood for Love. Anything I should know?"
  • Produces a public contribution link, plus a native share sheet.
  • The recipient needs no account. They land on a single field, write what they know, and submit. Optional name so the curator knows who to credit.
  • Contributions land in a From your experts tray next to the agent findings, in the same shape as findings, so either can be dragged into a note.

Deliberately not in V1, all noted by James or Michael as later: co-curator roles with admin control and a research role, cinephile levelling, genre badging, and a searchable expert bank. Michael drew that line on the call: "the V1 of that is what you just described. The V2 is inviting them in as like a co-curator."

6.4 Findings to note (1J③) · deferred

The brief in a readable column, grouped by scope. Highlighting any line turns it into a note draft, carrying its citation. The contributions tray sits in the same column, under its own heading. Add as note on any item opens the note editor with the text and source prefilled.

6.5 Set the note (1J④), the screen the product lives or dies on

The note editor. Body text, an optional attached still, and a fire time. This is where the curator spends their evening, and the call was clear that the fire time is the friction that decides whether the product is usable.

Four ways to set the fire time. Manual is always present and never removed.

  1. Manual. A mm:ss field. The base path. Everything else writes into this field, and the curator can always correct it.
  2. Screenshot. The curator uploads a frame from the film. The product identifies where that frame sits in the runtime and writes the timestamp. James on the flow: "so that person would just basically take a picture of their screen?" Yes. Curators are watching on a second or third screen while they prep.
  3. Natural language. A field that takes an instruction, not a number: "around 1h15, when Chow walks into the noodle stall, draft a note about the cheongsam." The product resolves the moment, writes a timestamp, and drafts note text the curator edits.
  4. From the queue. Nudge an existing note by plus or minus seconds without reopening it.

Implementation is a build-time investigation, not a solved problem. Screenshot to timestamp and language to timestamp are the two features most likely to slip. Candidate approaches, to be tested in this order: match against subtitle or script timing data for the film, which is cheap and accurate where the data exists; LLM vision against a frame plus scene data; and a fallback that returns a best-guess range with a confidence note rather than a false-precision timestamp. The UI must degrade honestly. If the product cannot place a frame, it says so and drops the curator into the manual field with whatever it inferred, rather than guessing silently.

Quote check is the easy win and ships first. Any quoted line in a note body gets a check action that verifies the wording against the script or subtitle data and offers the correction. James's use case verbatim: "I would quote a movie, I'm like, I don't know if I got that quote right, so can you tell me what the actual quote is?"

Still image. A note can carry an attached frame. The wireframes already show this as "Still attached, tap to change." The still is what the telestrator draws on during the session.

6.6 Note queue (1J⑤)

Every note for the session in fire order, each showing its mark, its first line, and whether it carries a still. Reorder by editing the mark, not by dragging rows, since fire time is the only real ordering. Intermissions live in the same list as a distinct row type, so the curator sees the shape of the night in one column. Add a new note, or pull one from research.

The pre-show sits at the top of the same column, on negative marks. −28:00 a TikTok, −20:00 a review, then 0:12 and 0:31 and the rest of the evening. One list, one ordering rule, and the whole night legible in a single scroll from doors to credits. Pre-show rows carry a link and a preview rather than body text, and they are pulled from her profile links (7.7) or pasted in for this film alone.

The editor rejects a pre-show mark later than −2:00. The last two minutes belong to the call, the curtain and the leader, and nothing is allowed to compete with them.

It is the same firing engine. A Liner Note fires when the session clock crosses its mark and a pre-show item fires when the countdown crosses its mark, which is the same clock read from the other side of zero. Building the second costs almost nothing once the first exists, and that is the argument for it landing in the same sprint.

Michael's requirement that the curator can improvise: "there should be the opportunity for them to edit and move things around as they want to, obviously, amidst the film." The queue is editable live during a session, not frozen at go-live.

Reserved space for the sponsored slot. The "this film brought to you by" bar and the sponsored Liner Note are not built yet, but the note row and the pre-show surface both reserve the slot so adding it later is not a redesign.

6.7 Go live and press play (1J⑥)

The launch screen. Curtains countdown, the note count armed, and one action: Go live and press play. Copy stays as the wireframes have it: "Cue the film on your TV. On zero, press play on both. Your notes fire from that moment."

The Howl is a checkbox on this screen: announce to social at zero.

6.8 On air (1J⑦)

The curator's live panel. Chat as the main column. Session clock and seat count. The next note with its countdown, notes firing automatically, and a manual fire override for drift. A compose field to drop a note now, unplanned. Controls for group pause, intermission, telestrator, and ending the film.

The action is End the film, not End session. Pressing it starts Lights up (7.4) rather than closing the room: the house comes up, chat stays open five minutes, the wrap Q&A opens in chat for her to answer live, and her next screening sits pinned above the compose field with a reserve action.

She does not paste a link mid-session. The handoff is set in prep (6.1) and fires on its own at minute three. During Lights up she sees what is about to appear and can clear it with one tap if she has changed her mind. Asking a host to find a URL with a room watching is asking her to fumble.

6.9 The promo kit (1J⑧)

When a session is created the curator gets a real screen with the link on it. Michael named this as the missing piece: "I create my event, then I should have a link that I can then share out and post on social." It is the first link in the entire chain and nothing in the audience journey happens without it.

It is also the highest-leverage surface in the product, because reach to reservation is half the funnel and the follower-to-attendee conversion rate is what the whole business turns on. So this screen is a promo kit rather than a copy button.

What it holds:

  • The share card, previewed as it will render when posted, per platform.
  • Per-channel tracked links. A distinct link for Instagram bio, story, TikTok, Substack, newsletter, X, and anywhere else the curator posts. This is the part that matters most, see below.
  • Draft posts per channel, editable, in the curator's own register rather than ours. Story text is not newsletter text.
  • A reminder sequence the curator can arm once: an announcement, a two-days-out nudge, a day-of, and an hour-of. Fired from here so she does not have to remember.
  • A results view after the screening: reach, reservations, and attendance broken out by channel.

Per-channel tracked links are the point. Three things follow from them that nothing else in the product delivers:

  1. The curator learns which of her channels actually converts. Telling someone her Substack converts six times better than her TikTok is an insight she cannot get anywhere else, and it is a reason to keep using the tool rather than posting a bare link.
  2. We get a real denominator for the conversion metric rather than an estimate. Reach is otherwise unmeasurable to us.
  3. Across a cohort it produces a per-channel conversion dataset, which is the thing that tells us who to recruit next and how to coach them.

Deliberately not a third-party integration. A scheduling-platform partnership was considered and deferred. It would cost engineering commitment, buy no metric, and hand measurement of our linchpin number to a company with no reason to give it back. The kit above is roughly a week of work and we own the funnel data. A partnership becomes interesting later as distribution, being present where film creators already are, once onboarding is a funnel rather than five conversations.

6.10 The confirmation state and the dry run (1J⑨)

Michael's open item: "they need to have some sort of screen that shows, okay, this is all in and completed and it worked."

Confirmation is the smaller half: a session ready summary showing the film, the showtime, the note count, the intermissions, and the share link, with anything unfinished called out.

The dry run is the proposal, and it is what makes a two-week curator beta possible with no audience. The curator presses a rehearse action and watches their own session play against a running clock, at real speed or accelerated, with their notes firing on their marks exactly as an audience member would see them. They can scrub to any mark, watch a single note land in context, and fix a mistimed note without leaving the screen.

This turns the beta from "fill in forms and hope" into "watch your show and adjust it," which is the only way five curators produce useful feedback before an audience exists. It also builds the note-firing engine that the audience app needs, so no work is thrown away.

7. Audience app

7.1 Onboarding (1e)

Three cards, per the wireframes: a curator hosts, live, on a schedule; the film plays on your TV; their Liner Notes land on your phone.

Then build your roster, following curators, and then where you watch, which is the streaming services the person actually has. That answer drives everything in 7.3.

No account required to continue. Optional Google or Apple sign-in, which buys following, push, and the Taste Passport, and nothing else. Reserving a seat needs an email address only.

7.2 The Marquee (1f)

What is on now, what is on tonight, what is on this week. Live cards ignite, everything else stays matte. Four tabs: Marquee, Roster, Notes, You.

7.3 Session detail and reserve a seat (1g)

The screen a shared link opens. Film, year, runtime, curator, showtime, countdown, the curator's one-sheet under "why I picked it," and the seat count with friends called out.

Make sure you can play it. Against the services declared at onboarding: where the film is available, at what price, with a deep link that opens the film in that app so the viewer can start it and AirPlay to the TV. James described the target experience: "it's all my phone, and now I'm AirPlaying Road House to the TV. And on my phone I've got the room."

JustWatch is the intended source. Michael confirmed on the call that the API exists at apis.justwatch.com and James owns the review of scope, licensing, and cost. Until that comes back, the fallback is manual: the curator enters where the film can be watched when they create the session, and the screen renders that. Build the manual path first regardless, since it is also the answer for any film JustWatch does not cover.

Reserve a seat, then two prompts on the way in, which are James's crowdsourcing idea:

  • "You like this movie. What do you have to add?"
  • "Anything you want to ask?"

Both optional, both skippable. Additions flow to the curator's contributions tray (6.3). Questions collect for the wrap Q&A on the recap screen. Michael's ordering: "the first prompt should be like, you like this movie, what do you have to add? And then beyond that, do you have any questions?"

7.4 The night (1d)

The Lobby, T-30. Doors open. Marquee board over open chat with the curator. Copy on the wireframe: "No notes till the Double Deuce opens. Settle in, cue your TV."

What else she is running. Her other dated screenings sit under the marquee board, above chat, each with a reserve action. Thirty minutes of waiting is the one stretch of the night where nothing is competing for attention, everyone in the room has already demonstrated they will turn up for this curator, and the film has not started so nobody is being interrupted. It is the best moment in the product to book a second night and it currently holds a countdown and small talk.

The rail collapses to a line when she has nothing else on the calendar, which will be true of most curators at the start of a season. It never covers the chat.

This is one component built once and placed three times: here at T-30, pinned above the compose field during Lights up (below), and on her profile (7.7). Same query, same card, same checkout. Two of the three sit either side of the film, which is deliberate: the room is warmest before the lights go down and just after they come up, and those are the two moments worth asking.

The pre-show. A cinema does not hold you in an empty room for half an hour. It runs something: trailers, slides, the local restaurant advert nobody admits to enjoying. The Lobby should work the same way, and the material is already sitting on the curator's profile.

She queues her own things against the countdown, exactly the way she queues Liner Notes against the film. A TikTok she made about this film at T-28. The review she wrote at T-20. A clip of the director talking, or last season's programme, or the trailer. Each one fires into the chat surface and plays there, embedded, rather than throwing anyone out to another app.

The rules that make it bearable rather than an advert break.

  • Nothing fires after T-2. The last call, the curtain and the leader own the final two minutes and nothing competes with them. The queue is over before the room starts counting down.
  • Muted, always, until someone taps. Everyone is in their own living room with a television already cued, not in a shared auditorium. Sound that arrives uninvited is hostile here in a way it is not in a cinema.
  • One embed on the surface at a time. A TikTok embed pulls the platform's player and roughly 120KB of script; three of them on a phone is a slow page and a warm handset half an hour before a film starts. The previous item unloads when the next fires.
  • Chat never leaves. Same rule as the Room. An item settles chat to a strip while it plays and chat comes back after, so the room can talk about what it just saw.
  • Skippable and re-openable. Anything that has fired stays in the chat stream as a card, so someone arriving at T-12 can scroll back through what they missed.

What embeds, and what that costs. Verified rather than assumed, because it decides what the queue can accept:

  • YouTube takes an iframe. Trailers, video essays, interviews.
  • TikTok has an oEmbed endpoint returning the official player. This is where most film creators actually publish.
  • Instagram dropped its access-token and app-review requirement on 15 June 2026, so a public post embeds tokenless. Single posts only, which is all this needs.
  • Anything else falls back to the link preview card the profile already uses (7.7): title, image, source, and a tap that opens it elsewhere.

Why she will use it. The embed is the platform's own player, so the view counts on her side. She is not giving us her back catalogue, she is playing it to a room that already turned up for her, thirty minutes before the thing they came for. That is a better audience than her feed gives her.

Rights, in one line. Her own work and things she is entitled to embed. An embed is the platform serving its own content, which is the reason to embed rather than rehost, and the same instinct that keeps us from touching the film.

The calls, T-10 and T-2. A notification, then a resting countdown page. There is no notification at T-1; the curtain appearing behind the chat is the one-minute call, and it is a better one because it cannot be missed by someone already looking at the screen. The full pre-show table is in DESIGN.md. This screen and the Lobby read as one to James: "I'm not sure I understand the difference between those two pages." The fix is to make the timing legible on the surfaces themselves rather than in a founder's head. If it still does not hold up in the curator beta, fold the calls into the Lobby as a state change. Michael was fine with that: "we could get rid of it too."

The leader, T-15 seconds. Rings, crosshair, sweep, one tick per second, counted down together.

T-0. The curtains part once, the ON AIR sign ignites, everyone presses play. The session clock starts.

The Room. Chat is the default surface and stays on screen, labeled THE ROOM — CHAT. Three layouts, which are one screen with alternates rather than three products: 1c chat forward is the default, 1a is the Liner Notes stream with its scrollable timestamped history, and 1b is one note at a time for someone who wants the room quiet. Swipe between them. Michael: "1A and 1B are alternative screens that you could swipe to, where you can be like, oh, I wanna go back and see what they said. It's not just buried in the chat."

A Liner Note arrives. It fades up like a subtitle, in the serif, holds about seven seconds, and chat minimizes to a strip while it holds. It also lands in the chat stream, so nothing is lost. No badges, no unread counters.

Intermission. Set in advance by the curator. Full screen, matte, a countdown, the curator's message, and an I've paused my TV action, which tells the curator how much of the room is actually with them. The honest limitation, still unresolved: the product cannot pause anyone's television. Michael flagged it as the hard problem, "how do we manage it without the technical integration," and James agreed it is technical. Until there is an answer, intermission is a social contract with good instrumentation, and the copy has to be explicit that the viewer pauses their own screen.

Telestrator (1d⑧). The curator's still with their ink on it, drawn live, holding until they release it. James: "is this the Madden chalkboard?" Chat minimizes while it holds.

Reel change (1d⑨). Whoever drifts sees what they missed, how far behind they are, and a Resync action. Never framed as an error. Michael: "you missed some time, what have you, but you're catching up."

Lights up (1d⑩). The credits roll and the house comes up. This is the lighting model doing its job: the show is over, the ground warms from house-down back to house-up, and the room is a lobby again rather than a dark auditorium.

Chat stays open for five minutes, and everything that used to be the recap happens inside it. People have just watched a film together and the worst thing the product can do is close the door on them at the last frame. The Liner Notes stop. Nothing else does.

The five minutes, in order.

WhenWhat
T+0:00The house comes up. Chat carries on, with a countdown that does not nag
T+0:00The wrap Q&A opens in the chat surface: the questions people left when they reserved, for the curator to answer live in front of the room that asked them
T+0:00Her next screening sits pinned above the compose field with a reserve action, so booking it is one tap while the film is still in the room's mouth
T+3:00The handoff banner appears in chat: come and carry on with me over here
T+5:00Chat closes. The recap page (7.5) is what remains

Putting the Q&A and the reserve action in the chat rather than on a page after it is the whole point. A question answered live, to the person who asked, in front of everyone, is a different thing from an answer read alone the next morning. And a reservation taken while the room is still talking about the film converts better than one asked for after everybody has left.

The handoff, at minute three. Two minutes is long enough to say goodbye and short enough that nobody has drifted off. The banner carries the curator's own live link, and it is a banner rather than a message because a message scrolls away from anyone who looks up late.

The curator sets that link once, in prep, not in the moment. She is hosting, and asking her to find and paste a URL with a room watching is asking her to fumble. The link lives on her session, defaults to whatever she used last time, and she can change or clear it up to the moment the banner fires.

What can actually be pre-set, which differs by platform. This decides the copy, so it is worth being exact:

  • TikTok. tiktok.com/@handle/live is a stable path that resolves to whatever she is broadcasting. Pre-settable, and it works.
  • YouTube. youtube.com/@handle/live is a permanent redirect to her current stream, and to her streams page when she is not live. Pre-settable, and it works.
  • Instagram. There is no equivalent. The stream key refreshes every session and there is no per-user live URL to hand anyone. The honest fallback is her profile, instagram.com/handle, which shows the live ring when she is on. The copy has to say where the link goes — "Naomi is going live on Instagram, this opens her profile" — rather than promising a stream and delivering a profile.

So the field takes a platform and a handle, builds the URL for the platforms where a permanent one exists, and accepts a raw URL for anything else. Where the link only reaches a profile, the banner says so.

Timing is derived, not stored. Lights up starts when the curator ends the film and runs five minutes from that moment, so a session that finishes late or early behaves correctly without anybody updating a field.

7.5 Recap and Film DNA (1h)

What is left when the room empties, rather than a surface competing with it. The live half of the recap moved into Lights up (7.4): the Q&A is answered in chat, in front of the people who asked, and the next screening is booked while the film is still being talked about.

So this page is the record. Who watched, the saved Liner Notes, the Q&A as it was answered, a rating, Film DNA as three tags for what to watch next, and her next session with a reserve action for anyone who arrives here later. It is what a link to the evening opens a week afterwards, and it is where someone who missed the five minutes catches up.

7.6 Taste Passport (1i)

A library card, not a trophy case. Curators followed, sessions attended, notes saved, proof you were in the room.

7.7 The curator profile (1k)

The Taste Passport's mirror image. Where the passport is what an audience member has sat through, the profile is what a curator has programmed, and it is the page a stranger lands on when they follow a link from her bio.

What it holds.

  • Who she is. Name, a line about what she programmes, and her social links out. These are links away from us on purpose.
  • What she has screened. The back catalogue, each with the date and the room size. This is the credential; a programmer with thirty screenings behind her does not have to argue that she can fill a room.
  • What is coming. Every dated screening with a reserve action, so the profile books seats rather than only describing her.
  • Things she wants you to see. Arbitrary links she chooses, each rendered as a preview with a title, an image and its source. A TikTok she made about a film, a review she wrote, a season she programmed at a repertory house. She is a critic with a body of work and this is the only place it sits together.

Why this is load-bearing rather than decorative. The promo kit's tracked links currently point at a single session page, which is the right target for one screening and the wrong one for a link in a bio. The profile is the durable destination: it survives between screenings, it converts a follower into a reservation for whatever is next, and it keeps feeding the per-channel conversion data the whole business turns on. Building it turns the promo kit from a per-event tool into a standing funnel.

Link previews are a background job, not something fetched while the page renders. The curator pastes a URL, a worker fetches the page, reads its Open Graph tags and stores a title, an image and a source. Unfurling fails often and slowly, so the profile has to render a plain link when a preview does not come back, and never a broken card.

Reserving from the profile reuses the session flow. Same page, same checkout, same tracked link. Nothing about payment is specific to this surface.

9. Technical requirements

Not a scaffold prescription. These are the constraints the frontend has to satisfy, whatever generates it.

  • Two surfaces, two form factors, and they are not the same product.
  • The audience side is a phone, held next to a television. Mobile first, designed at 390 by 844. It must also work on a laptop, since some people will watch on one screen, but the phone is the design target and the one that gets the attention.
  • The curator authoring tools are desktop, designed at 1440 by 900 and supported down to about 1280 wide. A curator writes notes with the film playing on a second screen. This is a laptop activity and the tools are built for it.
  • Authoring is desktop only, deliberately. A curator who opens prep, research, the note editor, the queue, the promo kit or the dry run on a phone gets an honest message asking them to come back on a laptop, not a cramped version of a professional tool. Building those screens responsive down to 390px doubles the front-end work for a use case that barely exists, and a squeezed note editor is worse than no note editor.
  • Hosting is the exception and must work on a phone. During a session the curator is watching the film like everyone else, and may well have the film on the television and Projector in their hand. Go live (1J⑥) and On air (1J⑦) need a phone layout: the note countdown, fire now, group pause, intermission, and end session, in reach of a thumb. Everything else in 1J does not.
  • Supabase for Postgres, Auth, and Realtime, with row level security on session and user data.
  • Sync model. Companion mode only, no player embed and no player API. The server tracks elapsed time from a shared start_time and notes fire when the session clock crosses their mark. Identical behavior no matter where the film is playing.
  • Auth. Every audience member gets an anonymous session on arrival, so user_id stays non-null across seats, chat, and reactions. Optional Google or Apple sign-in upgrades that same session through identity linking, carrying history forward. Curators require a Google-linked account and an anonymous session can never publish.
  • Research agent. A background job, not a real-time service. Approved-source lookups, then an LLM synthesis pass, then a written brief the curator dashboard polls for. Under five minutes per film.
  • Timestamp inference. A separate background service from the research agent. Takes a still or a natural language instruction plus the film identity and returns a timestamp with a confidence value. Must be able to return "not confident" as a first-class result.
  • Payments. Stripe, with the Payment Request Button so Apple Pay and Google Pay are offered wherever the device supports them and a card field is the fallback. At $0.99 the friction of typing a card is larger than the price, which is the whole argument for the wallets.
  • Link previews. A background worker that fetches a URL, reads its Open Graph tags and stores a title, an image and a source. It must be able to fail and say so; a profile renders a plain link rather than a broken card.
  • Embeds. YouTube by iframe, TikTok and Instagram by their oEmbed endpoints, everything else by the preview card above. Resolve the embed when the curator adds the item, not when it fires, so a slow provider cannot stall the room. One player mounted at a time: unmount the previous item before the next fires, because these SDKs are heavy and the audience is on a phone.
  • Email for reminders and expert asks. Resend or equivalent.

10. Build sequence

Reordered so the curator beta can start without an audience app, and scheduled against the six sprints in Projector-Sprint-Plan.md. Dates are the plan of 27 August 2026.

Two things are cut from everything below. The research agent (6.2, 6.4) and AI timestamping (6.5, ways two and three) do not appear in any sprint before the raise. Manual mm:ss is the base path and it is sufficient for the beta. Both come back after there is an engineer who can build a background job properly, and a half-built version of either is worse than nothing to that engineer.

Who builds what. A contract engineer sets up the repository, schema, migrations and row-level security first, then builds the realtime spine: the session clock, the note-firing engine, and chat. Michael builds schema-adjacent work, forms, CRUD, layout and routing with coding agents inside that. The split is deliberate. The spine is the part generated code produces convincingly and wrongly, and the part a curator's evening depends on.

Michael. Supabase project. The section 9 schema. Row-level security on every table. Google OAuth for curators, no email and password path. Session creation. Prep home (1J①) with Go live disabled.

Acceptance. A curator signs in, creates a session, and it persists across a reload.

Sprint 2 · 14 Sep to 25 Sep · The authoring loop

Michael. Note editor (1J④) with body text, still upload, and manual mm:ss only. Note queue (1J⑤) with intermission rows and the plus or minus five second nudge. Intermission creation. Engineer. The session clock, server-authoritative, resilient to a locked phone or a backgrounded tab. The note-firing engine, with a manual override. The realtime channel that the dry run and the Room both sit on.

After the raise

The curator profile (1K) comes first of these, because it is the only thing on the list that earns money rather than costing it. It is where a link in a bio should point, it books seats between screenings, and it turns the promo kit from a per-event tool into a standing funnel. It needs an artboard drawn before it is built. Its link previews are a background job and inherit whatever the research agent's worker turns out to be.

Then everything else deferred above, in this order: the research agent and its source discovery, AI timestamping by screenshot and by natural language, quote check, ask other experts, the telestrator, group pause, reel change and resync, the Marquee, the recap with wrap Q&A and Film DNA, the Taste Passport, push notifications, and JustWatch if the review comes back green.

What gets cut first when a sprint runs long

In this order, and do not improvise a different one: the dry run's speed toggle; the pre-show, which is the richest thing in the Lobby and the only part needing embed handling, leaving the doors, the chat and the screenings rail intact; then the Lobby itself, which by then costs a booking moment as well as the arrival, though Lights up keeps the same rail on the other side of the film; the handoff banner inside Lights up, keeping the five minutes of chat, the Q&A and the reserve action, because those three are where the value is and the banner is the only part that needs a curator to have set something up; On air on a phone (1J⑦), which means the founding five host from a laptop and is survivable at five curators but not at fifty; the promo kit's draft posts and reminder sequence keeping the tracked links; the recap; the Marquee.

Never cut: row-level security, the design language pass, the tracked links, the desktop-only guard, or the engineer's failure states. The guard is twenty minutes of work, and without it a curator opening the note editor on a train meets a broken screen as her first impression of the product.

11. Open questions

Intermission without integration. Unresolved on the call and the single biggest hole in companion mode. The product cannot pause a television. Instrumentation plus explicit copy is the current answer and it is a social contract, not a mechanism.

Timestamp inference feasibility. Which films have usable subtitle or script timing data, what vision costs per lookup, and what accuracy is good enough to be worth the button. Answer this after the raise with real films, not in advance.

JustWatch. Scope, licensing, and cost, with James. Manual entry ships either way.

The Lobby and the calls. Two screens or one. Let the curator beta and the first real session decide.
