1. Title

PROJECTOR

Watch films with the people who love them.

Pre-seed · August 2026 · James DeJulio and Michael Martin

Visual: house up. Warm ambient on near black, no visible source. No product screenshot here.

2. The problem

Streaming gave everyone everything and took away the one thing that made it worth watching.

Nobody is in the room with you.

Three things broke at once:

For the viewer. A thousand hours of catalog and no reason to pick anything. The algorithm optimizes for the next thing you will tolerate, not the thing you will remember. Watching alone at home is the default and it is worse than what it replaced.

For the film. Everyone talks about shows. Movies get buried. A studio spends nine figures on a library that nobody can find a way into, and a catalog title has no path back to an audience once its window closes.

For the person with taste. A film programmer with 800,000 followers has a job at a theater and a Letterboxd account. There is no product that turns deep knowledge of film into a live event with an audience and an income. They are the only people in the culture whose expertise has no marketplace.

Speaker note: the third one is the wedge. The first two are why the third one matters.

3. The insight

The missing layer was never a better algorithm. It was a person.

Every attempt to fix discovery has been machine-first: better recommendations, better rows, better thumbnails. The one thing that reliably makes someone watch a film they have never heard of is another human being they trust saying watch this, and then sitting next to them while it plays.

That person exists. They have an audience already. What they do not have is a room, a schedule, and a way to get paid.

Projector is the room.

4. The product

A curator picks a film, sets a showtime, and hosts a screening. You watch on your own TV. Their notes arrive on your phone, in sync, alongside everyone else in the room.

Think Pop-Up Video, live, with a film programmer instead of a producer and a hundred people instead of you alone on the couch.

The architectural decision that makes it work: we never touch the film.

No player. No embed. No streaming rights. No platform integration. The viewer presses play on whatever service they already pay for, and Projector runs on a shared start time. That means every film ever made is already in the catalog on day one, and it means we can launch without a single deal.

Visual: the two-device diagram. Television with the film, phone with the room. This is the slide that needs the wireframes.

5. How a session works

Before. The curator researches the film with our tools, writes Liner Notes pinned to specific moments, and posts a link. The audience reserves seats and, on the way in, tells the curator what they know and what they want to ask.

Doors. Thirty minutes before showtime the lobby opens and the room starts talking.

Showtime. A fifteen second leader counts down, the curtains part, everyone presses play together.

During. Liner Notes fire on their marks. A scene is a filming location, a line was improvised, the grip on this picture ran second unit on something you love. Chat runs the whole time. The curator can pause the room for an intermission or draw on a still to show you what to look at.

After. A recap holds every note, the questions get answered, and the next screening is already on the board.

Visual: the sequence, five beats across the slide. Pull the actual screens.

6. Why now

Human curation already out-earns the algorithm. It just has no product.

Letterboxd has roughly 17 million members organizing their entire cultural life around what other people think of films. It is a review site with no live layer, no schedule, and no way for anyone on it to make a living. It is proof of appetite, not a competitor. [verify current number]

The Rewatchables is a top-ten entertainment podcast whose entire format is a few people talking about a film you have already seen. Millions listen to the conversation without ever watching along. The demand for company is so strong that the audience accepts a version where nobody is actually watching together.

Karina Longworth built You Must Remember This into a Netflix-adjacent institution by explaining films nobody had thought about in thirty years. She announced a new season this month. She has never once been able to screen the films she talks about.

Cinema How, film students in England, has 821,000 followers for scene breakdowns on Instagram. Color theory in Pixar. Blocking in The Godfather. They are film programmers who have never been given a theater.

Secondary evidence of the same appetite: NTS Radio's monthly audience choosing human curation over algorithmic radio, seventeen consecutive years of vinyl sales growth, and a four billion dollar podcast advertising market built on the premise that a trusted voice moves behavior better than a display ad. [verify all three]

And the supply side just changed. Repertory programming is having a genuine cultural moment while the theaters that host it keep closing. The people who can do this work are more visible and less employed than at any point in the last twenty years.

7. Why this wins

Map the market on two axes: who is choosing, and when it happens.

On demandLive
Algorithm choosesNetflix, SpotifyTikTok
Human choosesLetterboxd, Substack, CriterionProjector

Human plus Live is empty. Teleparty, Scener, and Kast are utilities for watching with three friends, with no curator, no schedule, no audience, and no money in them. Nobody owns the quadrant where a person with taste programs a night and gets paid for it.

The moat is the thing that looks like a weakness.

Because we never rebroadcast the film, we are not competing with anyone for rights, and we are the cleanest counterparty a studio or a streamer will meet. The instructive comparison: Twitch is enormous and owned by Amazon and still cannot get an NFL deal, because technically Twitch rebroadcasts. We do not. Every viewer loads the film from the source they already pay for.

That makes us a distribution partner rather than a threat, and it is the reason a conversation with a rights holder starts at what can we do together instead of what are you doing with our content.

Rights partnerships are an enabler, not a revenue line. A cleared title lets us run a marquee screening. It is worth having and it is not the business, and we do not need one to launch. Do not confuse it with licensing the tooling, which is motion two on the next slide and is a different conversation with some of the same companies.

The second moat is the curator. Their audience trust took years to build and we cannot manufacture it. What we can do is be the only place that trust converts into a live event and an income, which is why the first five matter more than the first five thousand.

8. Business model

Two motions. The creator motion proves the behaviour. The enterprise motion carries the scale.

Motion one: the creator

Ticketed screenings. A seat costs $0.99 and the curator keeps seventy percent of it, which is the rate Cameo pays and the rate a person with an audience already expects. The arithmetic below rounds the ticket to a dollar.

At one and a half percent of a hundred thousand followers converting, that is a room of 1,500 and about $1,050 a screening to her, $450 to us. At weekly cadence, roughly $55,000 a year to her and $23,000 to us per active mid-size curator. For Cinema How at 821,000 followers, one percent is a room of 8,200 and about $5,700 a screening to the curator.

We take our thirty percent on the web, which is the whole reason the number holds. Cameo is the cautionary version: an iOS booking hands Apple thirty percent first, and the creator's share comes out of what is left, so a dollar becomes about fifty cents. Reserving a seat here is a web checkout, not an in-app purchase, and since May 2025 the US App Store charges nothing on external payment links either. Shipping a native app with in-app purchase would delete a third of the curator's income and a third of ours, which is a product decision disguised as a distribution one.

That conversion rate is the hinge for the whole company. It is our metric and it is also the curator's paycheque, and the two fail together.

Interactive sponsorship. A sponsor in a live room is not a banner and not a promo code read into a microphone. It is the curator taking the room to the sponsor at intermission, with everyone arriving at once and able to act. The comparison is a podcast promo code, where the conversion rate is a rounding error. We expect this to be the higher margin line well before ticket volume matters.

Motion two: licensing the tooling

Organisations that already have talent and audiences, and no live layer, run their own screenings on our tools and pay for the software and a share.

  • Publishers. The Ringer and its peers. The Rewatchables is already this format, done worse, with no way for the audience to actually watch along. It is our first target and we have a direct path to their editorial director.
  • Streamers and distributors. Tubi, MUBI, A24, Neon. MUBI's entire brand is curation with no live layer. A24 already runs a membership and repertory screenings by hand.
  • Festivals. Thousands globally, all programming already, all trying to reach past the people who can physically attend.
  • Repertory and arthouse cinemas. Hundreds, programming weekly, with an audience and no way past the room. A handful of them decide the category. If Metrograph and the American Cinematheque are on this, the argument is over for everyone else, because those two are how the field decides what is serious. That is the recruiting target, not a number of logos.

Deal sizes here are an order of magnitude above a single curator, the margins are better, and it is contracts rather than a churning roster. This is the motion James opens.

Speaker note: lead with motion one because it is the one we can prove this quarter. Motion two is named organisations and a warm path into the first one, which is access rather than traction. Say it that way. The moment the Ringer meeting actually happens it moves to the proof of concept slide, and until then it does not live there.

8b. What the curator actually gets

The curator is the customer. She does not pay us, and she is still the one who has to be convinced, because nothing in this business exists until somebody agrees to programme a night. So the offer has to be worth her evening on more than one axis, and money is only the first.

Money. Seventy percent of the door on an audience she already has, for work she is currently doing free. A mid-size curator clears about a thousand dollars on a screening she would have done anyway.

Her own numbers. Every channel she posts to gets a distinct tracked link, so she learns which of them actually fills a room. Telling someone her Substack converts nine times better than her TikTok is a fact nobody else in her life can give her, and it changes what she does next week.

A back catalogue that earns twice. The film essay she already made plays to a room of two hundred people thirty minutes before the film they came for. The embed is the platform's own player, so the view counts on her side. She is not giving us her work; she is screening it to a warm audience.

Audience she keeps. The five minutes after the credits hand the room to her live stream, and her profile is the thing a link in her bio should point at. Some of that room came from our audience rather than hers, and she leaves with them. James's framing: eventually you want to bring the app's audience with you onto your own social.

A room, and a reason to have a point of view in it. She is a programmer without a theatre. This is the theatre, and the notes are the thing only she can write.

Standing. The first names on this platform define what it is. There is a small number of institutions whose presence settles the question for everyone else, and being early is worth something to a person whose currency is taste.

Speaker note: this is the slide to slow down on with anyone who asks why curators would bother. The answer is not "a thousand dollars." It is that four of these six do not exist anywhere else at any price.

The unit. A curator with a hundred thousand followers, converting at one and a half percent, at $0.99 a seat, running weekly: 1,500 attendees a screening, seventy-eight thousand a year in gross tickets, about twenty-three thousand of net revenue to us per active mid-size curator, and fifty-five thousand to her.

The supply universe. The compiled, segmented list of people already doing film criticism and film content: video essayists, film TikTok and Instagram, Letterboxd writers, film podcast hosts, Substack critics, repertory programmers. [insert the real list, segmented by audience size]

The licensing pool. A named count of publishers, streamers, distributors, festivals, rep houses and film programmes, at low six figures a year each. [insert the count] This pool is comparable to the entire creator line, at better margin.

And the ceiling is not film. Nothing in the architecture is film-specific. A shared start time, notes on marks, a room, and never touching the content works identically for a television episode, and television is where the attention is. Film is the wedge. Timed media is the market.

10. Go to market

Five curators. Session data. Streamer deals. In that order.

The supply side unlocks everything else. Without curators there is no product and no pitch. With five credentialed curators and real session data, the first streamer conversation is a data presentation rather than a cold ask.

Who we recruit. People with a documented film audience and no way to monetize their taste as an event. Instagram and TikTok film essayists, repertory programmers at named independent theaters, film podcast hosts, and Letterboxd writers with real followings.

What we offer. No fee, and for the founding cohort no revenue share either: they keep one hundred percent of ticket sales across their first three screenings, then seventy percent, which is also the standard rate. Plus founding credit, brokered introductions to streaming editorial, and their own per-channel conversion data, which nobody else can give them.

What we ask. Three dated screenings. Not a trial. Return rate does not exist below two, and it is the number this whole business turns on.

How we recruit, in three motions. Founder-led outreach gets the first twenty-five, one at a time, from a team they have reason to take a call with. After that, outbound against a compiled and segmented universe of people already doing film criticism and film content: video essayists, film TikTok and Instagram, Letterboxd writers, film podcast hosts, Substack critics, repertory programmers. Then curator referral, on revenue share, once the product sells itself peer to peer.

First names on the list. Cinema How, 821,000 followers, an existing working relationship with James. Karina Longworth, whose new season just landed. The Rewatchables format as the model for what a monthly screening looks like at scale.

11. The proof of concept

What we are running right now, and what it produces.

Phase one, the tools. Five curator design partners get the authoring tools and roughly two weeks to break them. They research films, write Liner Notes, and rehearse full sessions against a running clock with no audience present. Output is a product that survives contact with the people who will actually use it.

Phase two, the screenings. Each of the five runs roughly one screening a week. Small on purpose. Every session is a case study.

The number that decides the company is the third one. If a curator can convert even a low single digit percentage of their existing following into people who show up at a specific time on a specific night, the model works and every other number is a matter of scale.

13. Roadmap

Now. Curator tools built. Five design partners recruited and testing.

Next 90 days. Screenings running weekly. Audience app live. First session data. First conversations with a streamer or studio about a cleared title for a marquee screening.

Six to twelve months. Curator roster past twenty. Ticketing live. First sponsored screening. First negotiated title with a partner.

Twelve to eighteen months. Curators running recurring channels with their own audiences. Sponsorship as a repeatable product. Attribution data good enough to price a partnership on performance rather than a flat fee.
